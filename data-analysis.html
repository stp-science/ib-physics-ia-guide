    What you need to know

    The core analysis skills

    You do not need to use every technique on this page. For most IAs, focus on the seven core steps below and only use the optional extras when they genuinely help your investigation.

    1UncertaintyEstimate measurement uncertainty honestly.
    2RepeatsUse a mean and describe the spread.

    Step 1

    Measurement uncertainty

    Every measured quantity has uncertainty. Your job is not to make the uncertainty tiny; it is to estimate it honestly and use it consistently.

    Instrument resolution

    Use the smallest meaningful change the instrument can resolve as your minimum uncertainty unless your teacher has given a different convention or the manufacturer specifies one.

    Repeated readings

    If repeats vary by more than the instrument resolution, the spread of the repeats may be a better estimate of uncertainty.

    Digital sensors

    Do not assume a displayed value is exact. Consider sensor resolution, calibration and the variability of the reading.

    Timing

    For hand timing, reaction and judging the start/end often matter more than the stopwatch's displayed precision.

    Absolute and percentage uncertainty

    x=xmeasured±Δx
    Percentage uncertainty=Δxx×100%

    Example

    A length is measured as 0.842 m ± 0.002 m.

    Percentage uncertainty = (0.002 / 0.842) × 100 = 0.24%.

    Precision rule

    Round the uncertainty sensibly, usually to one significant figure (sometimes two when useful), then round the measured value to the same decimal place.

    Good

    1.24 ± 0.03 m

    Not consistent

    1.2422 ± 0.03 m

    Step 2

    Repeated measurements

    Repeats help you judge random variation. Do not just calculate a mean and throw away the spread.

    Mean

    x¯=Σxn

    Half-range uncertainty

    Δx=xmax−xmin2

    This is simple and useful when you have a small set of repeats. Your supplied IA guidance also allows standard deviation when it is appropriate.

    Minimum uncertainty: if all repeats are identical, the uncertainty is not zero. The instrument resolution still sets a minimum.
    Optional: when might I use standard deviation?
    Standard deviation describes the spread of repeated measurements around the mean. It can be useful when you have enough repeats and the spread is genuinely random. Do not use it just to make the analysis look more sophisticated.

    Example repeats

    0.74, 0.76, 0.75, 0.73, 0.75 m

    Mean = 0.746 m

    Half-range = (0.76 − 0.73)/2 = 0.015 m

    Step 3

    Uncertainty in calculated quantities

    When you calculate a new quantity from measured values, the uncertainties in those measurements affect the result. The rules below are a common conservative school-physics approach. If your teacher has taught a different approved method, use that consistently.

    CalculationUncertainty ruleExample
    Add or subtract
    Q = A ± B    		Add the absolute uncertainties.ΔQ = ΔA + ΔB
    Multiply or divide
    Q = AB or A/B    		Add the fractional or percentage uncertainties.ΔQ/Q = ΔA/A + ΔB/B
    Power
    Q = An    		Multiply the fractional uncertainty by the magnitude of the power.ΔQ/Q = |n| ΔA/A
    Exact constant
    Q = 2A    		An exact constant does not add measurement uncertainty.Percentage uncertainty in Q is the same as in A.
    Why percentage uncertainty matters: it lets you compare the size of uncertainty across quantities with different units and scales.

    Example: coefficient of restitution

    For e = √(hr/hd), the square root is a power of one-half:

    Δee=12Δhrhr+Δhdhd

    Step 4

    Error bars

    Error bars show the uncertainty of each plotted value. They are not decoration — they help you judge how strongly the data support a trend or model.

    Which error bars should I add?

    • Y error bars: use the uncertainty in your dependent or processed y-value.
    • X error bars: include them when the uncertainty in the independent variable is large enough to matter.
    • If every point has a different uncertainty, use custom error bars where your software allows it.
    • Do not choose a default “5% error” or “standard deviation” setting unless that actually matches how your uncertainty was determined.
    Extra help: adding custom error bars in Excel
    1. Create an XY scatter graph using your processed x and y values.
    2. Click the data series on the graph.
    3. Go to Chart Design → Add Chart Element → Error Bars → More Error Bar Options.
    4. Choose Custom → Specify Value.
    5. Select the cells containing your positive uncertainty values, then the negative values. For symmetric uncertainties these will normally be the same cells.
    6. Repeat for horizontal error bars if x-uncertainty is important.

    Google Sheets: only use its built-in error-bar options when they correctly represent your experimental uncertainty. If your points have different custom uncertainties and Sheets cannot display them correctly, use Excel, Logger Pro or another tool that supports per-point error bars.

    How to read error bars

    • Large bars mean the measurement gives weaker discrimination between nearby values or models.
    • A best-fit line does not need to pass through every error bar.
    • Overlapping error bars do not automatically prove two measurements are the same.
    • Look for systematic patterns: for example, all points lying above a model line at one end of the range.

    Step 5

    Choosing and making the graph

    The graph should test the physics relationship, not just make the data look neat.

    Axes

    Independent variable on x. Dependent or processed variable on y. Give both a quantity and a unit.

    Scatter graph

    Use an XY scatter graph for continuous numerical data. Do not use a category line chart.

    Best-fit model

    Choose linear, power, inverse or another model because the physics predicts it — not because it gives a high R².

    Origin

    Do not force the fit through the origin unless theory predicts zero intercept and the data are consistent with that within uncertainty.

    Using one of the suggested practicals? The Practical Ideas page already shows the useful relationship and graph for each investigation, so it is not repeated here.
    R² is not the conclusion. A high R² tells you the chosen fit follows the data closely. It does not by itself show that the physics model is correct.
    Optional: residuals
    A residual is measured value − value predicted by the fit. Randomly scattered residuals support the chosen model. A clear pattern can suggest that the model is missing something.

    Step 6

    Gradient and intercept

    Finding a gradient

    m=ΔyΔx

    Use two well-separated points on the line of best fit, not simply the first and last measured data points.

    Uncertainty in the gradient

    If your graphing method uses maximum and minimum acceptable lines:

    Δm=mmax−mmin2

    Then report the gradient as m ± Δm. If software gives a justified uncertainty in the fitted gradient, you may use that instead — explain what it represents.

    Use the gradient physically

    Do not stop at “the gradient is 171”. State what the gradient means.

    For a resonance graph of ΔL against 1/f:

    gradient = v/2, so v = 2 × gradient.

    Intercepts can be evidence

    If theory predicts a zero intercept but your best fit gives a clearly non-zero value outside uncertainty, do not hide it. It may be evidence of a systematic offset, an unaccounted physical effect or a limitation in the model.

    Judgement

    Outliers

    Do not remove a point because it makes the graph look worse.

    1. Check the original measurement and calculation.
    2. Repeat that condition if you still can.
    3. Look for a specific experimental reason for the unusual value.
    4. If you remove it, identify it and justify the removal.
    5. When useful, compare the analysis with and without the point.

    Step 7

    Comparing with theory or an accepted value

    Percentage difference

    Percentage difference=|xexp−xaccepted|xaccepted×100%

    Do not report this number on its own. Compare the difference with the uncertainty of your experimental result and discuss whether the result is consistent with the accepted value within the limits of the experiment.

    Cite the accepted value. If you compare your result with a physical constant or published value, use a reliable source and include it in your references.

    Putting it together

    Mini worked example: resonance tube

    Suppose a student plots ΔL against 1/f and obtains:

    m=171.5±4.0 m s⁻¹

    From the theory gradient = v/2, so:

    v=2m=343±8 m s⁻¹

    A strong analysis then does three things:

    1. States the experimental value and uncertainty.
    2. Compares it with an accepted speed of sound appropriate to the room conditions.
    3. Uses the graph, error bars and intercept to judge whether any disagreement is random scatter or evidence of a systematic effect.

    Assessed exemplar comparison

    The difference between 6/6 and 5/6 in Data Analysis

    This is a useful comparison because the 5/6 investigation still had good data and a good graph. The missing mark came from the treatment and communication of uncertainty, not from a lack of calculations.

    6 / 6Harmonic motion oscillations

    What the full-mark work did

    • Recorded and processed data clearly and concisely.
    • Used a formal and appropriate treatment of experimental uncertainty throughout.
    • Used processing that directly answered the research aim.
    • Separated the best-fit graph from the maximum/minimum gradient graph, making the uncertainty analysis easy to follow.
    • Connected errors and uncertainties to the significance of the final result.

    Actual student work - short extract

    “Standard deviation does not consider any experimental uncertainty, only a statistical scatter. Therefore, for the experimental uncertainty in the gradient I constructed ... minimum and maximum gradient lines by eye considering all the error bars.”

    The student distinguishes statistical scatter from experimental uncertainty and then changes the analysis accordingly.

    Assessor emphasis: clear and precise communication, excellent uncertainty treatment and graphs that directly addressed the research aim.

    5 / 6Dynamics of a spring

    Why a strong analysis still lost a mark

    • The tables and processing were clear, and the main graph was relevant.
    • Understanding of uncertainty was inconsistent, with omissions and inaccuracies.
    • Some force uncertainty bars were not properly justified.
    • The gradient uncertainty was not determined from experimental uncertainty using acceptable maximum/minimum lines.
    • The work relied on a statistical uncertainty from graphing software instead of clearly relating the gradient range to the experimental uncertainties.

    Actual student work - short extract

    “However, as 0.1 cm could not be seen on the graph, I adjusted the error bar to 0.5 cm in order to see it on the graph; the force uncertainty average of 5.9% was graphed.”

    This is the key problem: an error bar should represent a justified uncertainty. It should not be enlarged simply to make it visible.

    Assessor emphasis: the essence was strong enough for 5/6, but uncertainty handling was not consistently rigorous enough for a solid 6.

    What should you learn from this?

    Error bars appearing on a graph are not enough. You must be able to explain where each uncertainty came from, why it is appropriate, and how it affects the fitted relationship, gradient or final quantity.
