Practical ideas

Seven practical families based on physics already covered. Each uses manageable equipment but gives you scope to analyse a model, uncertainty and limitations rather than simply “prove an equation”.

Rolling ballRampBouncing ballPendulumStanding wavesResonance tubeRefraction
Important: these are starting points, not seven questions everyone should copy. Pilot the setup, discuss your idea with your teacher and refine the range or analysis so the question suits your own investigation. Do not add complexity simply to make the topic look original.
PRACTICAL 01 • A.1 / A.3 / A.4

Rolling ball: energy and release height

Use a simple ramp but analyse where the gravitational energy goes.

Recommended research questionHow does the vertical release height of a solid sphere affect the fraction of its initial gravitational potential energy converted into translational kinetic energy at the bottom of a fixed ramp?

Equipment

Ramp, solid sphere, metre rule, balance, blocks/stand, tape or markers, light gate or slow-motion video with a scale.

Key physics

\[\eta=\frac{\tfrac12 mv^2}{mgh}=\frac{v^2}{2gh}\]

For an ideal solid sphere rolling without slipping, the translational fraction is predicted to be \(\tfrac{5}{7}\).

Method

  1. Fix the ramp angle and mark the point where speed will be measured near the bottom.
  2. Choose a release point and measure its vertical height above the bottom measurement point.
  3. Release the same sphere from rest without pushing it.
  4. Measure its speed at the bottom using a light gate or calibrated slow-motion video.
  5. Repeat the measurement several times and find the mean speed.
  6. Repeat for at least six well-spaced vertical release heights.
  7. Calculate η for each height and include appropriate uncertainties.

Useful analysis

Plot η against release height. Compare the measured values and any trend with the ideal 5/7 prediction. Discuss rolling resistance, slipping, uncertainty in height and how the speed measurement affects η.

IV: vertical release heightDV: energy fraction ηKeep ramp + ball fixed
PRACTICAL 02 • A.1 / A.2

Trolley on a ramp: real vs ideal acceleration

Instead of only verifying a = g sin θ, investigate how the real system departs from the model.

Recommended research questionHow does incline angle affect the percentage difference between the measured acceleration of a trolley and the theoretical frictionless value g sin θ?

Equipment

Dynamics trolley, ramp, blocks/stand, inclinometer or protractor, motion sensor / light gates / video analysis, metre rule.

Key physics

\[a_{\mathrm{theory}}=g\sin\theta\]

Percentage difference lets you investigate whether the model becomes more or less appropriate as the angle changes.

Method

  1. Set the ramp to a small angle and measure θ accurately.
  2. Use the same trolley and the same release position for every run.
  3. Release the trolley from rest without pushing it.
  4. Determine acceleration using your chosen sensor or video method.
  5. Repeat and calculate a mean acceleration.
  6. Repeat for at least six ramp angles across a safe, practical range.
  7. Calculate \(g\sin\theta\) and the percentage difference for each angle.

Useful analysis

Plot measured acceleration against \(\sin\theta\) and/or percentage difference against angle. A non-zero intercept or systematic pattern can provide evidence about rolling resistance or other model limitations.

IV: incline angleDV: % differenceSame trolley + track
PRACTICAL 03 • A.1 / A.3

Bouncing ball: coefficient of restitution

Simple setup, but enough quantitative analysis to investigate how collision behaviour changes.

Recommended research questionHow does the initial drop height of a basketball affect its coefficient of restitution when bouncing from a fixed hard surface?

Equipment

Ball, vertical measuring scale, phone with slow-motion video, tripod/support, flat hard surface.

Key physics

\[e=\sqrt{\frac{h_{\mathrm{rebound}}}{h_{\mathrm{drop}}}}\]

You can also examine the fraction of gravitational energy retained after the first bounce.

Method

  1. Fix a vertical scale beside the bounce position and place the camera perpendicular to the motion.
  2. Choose a drop height and use the same reference point on the ball each time.
  3. Release the ball without spin or an extra push.
  4. Use the video to measure the maximum height of the first rebound.
  5. Repeat several times at the same height.
  6. Repeat for at least six drop heights.
  7. Calculate e and its uncertainty for each height.

Useful analysis

Plot e against drop height and decide whether it is approximately constant over your range. An alternative student question could investigate ball pressure rather than drop height if a pressure gauge is available.

IV: drop heightDV: coefficient of restitutionSame ball + surface
PRACTICAL 04 • C.1

Pendulum: where the small-angle model breaks down

Use the familiar pendulum in a less routine way by testing the limit of a model.

Recommended research questionHow does initial angular displacement affect the percentage deviation of the measured period of a simple pendulum from the period predicted by the small-angle approximation?

Equipment

Retort stand and clamp, string, pendulum bob, metre rule, protractor, stopwatch or preferably video.

Key physics

\[T_0=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}}\]

The standard expression assumes small angular displacement. Larger angles let you test where that approximation becomes less successful.

Method

  1. Build a pendulum with fixed length L and measure L from pivot to the centre of the bob.
  2. Choose a starting angle, beginning with a small value such as 5°.
  3. Release the bob without pushing it.
  4. Measure the time for multiple complete oscillations and calculate the period.
  5. Repeat to obtain a reliable mean period.
  6. Repeat for a wide range of initial angles, extending beyond the small-angle range.
  7. Calculate the percentage deviation from \(T_0\) at each angle.

Useful analysis

Plot percentage deviation against initial angle. Look for the region where deviation becomes larger than the experimental uncertainty rather than claiming one exact “failure angle”.

IV: initial angleDV: % deviation from T₀Fixed length + bob
PRACTICAL 05 • C.2 / C.4

Standing waves: harmonics at fixed frequency

Avoid the heavily used “frequency vs tension” question while keeping the same straightforward apparatus.

Recommended research questionHow does harmonic number affect the tension required to produce a standing wave on a string of fixed vibrating length at a fixed driving frequency?

Equipment

Signal generator, vibration generator, string, pulley, mass hanger and masses, metre rule, balance if linear density must be measured.

Key physics

\[f=\frac{n}{2L}\sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad T\propto\frac{1}{n^2}\]

At fixed f, L and μ, different harmonics require different string tensions.

Method

  1. Set a fixed vibrating length of string between the vibrator and pulley.
  2. Choose and keep one driving frequency throughout.
  3. Adjust the hanging mass until a clear standing-wave pattern is produced for a chosen harmonic number n.
  4. Record the hanging mass and calculate the tension \(T\approx mg\).
  5. Fine-adjust around resonance and repeat the measurement.
  6. Repeat for several harmonic numbers that can be produced clearly.
  7. Keep string type, vibrating length and driving frequency unchanged.

Useful analysis

Plot \(T\) against \(1/n^2\). A straight-line relationship is predicted. Discuss how accurately resonance can be judged, pulley friction, uncertainty in vibrating length and whether the string remains under uniform tension.

IV: harmonic number nDV: required tensionFixed f, L and μ
PRACTICAL 06 • C.2 / C.4

Resonance tube: spacing between resonances

Using two resonant lengths reduces the effect of the uncertain open-end correction.

Recommended research questionHow does driving frequency affect the separation between the first and second resonant lengths of a closed air column?

Equipment

Resonance tube with adjustable water level, metre rule, speaker/phone, signal generator or tone-generator app, stand/clamp.

Key physics

\[\Delta L=L_2-L_1=\frac{\lambda}{2}=\frac{v}{2f}\]

Because the same end correction appears in both resonant lengths, subtracting them largely removes it.

Method

  1. Choose a driving frequency and position the speaker close to the open end of the tube.
  2. Change the air-column length slowly until the first clear resonance is found; record \(L_1\).
  3. Continue changing the length until the next clear resonance is found; record \(L_2\).
  4. Repeat the pair of measurements to estimate the uncertainty in locating resonance.
  5. Calculate \(\Delta L=L_2-L_1\).
  6. Repeat for several frequencies for which both resonances fit within the tube.
  7. Keep the same tube and speaker arrangement.

Useful analysis

Plot \(\Delta L\) against \(1/f\). The gradient should be approximately \(v/2\), allowing you to obtain an experimental speed of sound and compare it with an accepted value appropriate to the room conditions.

IV: frequencyDV: ΔLSame tube + conditions
PRACTICAL 07 • C.3

Refraction: lateral displacement through one block

No collection of different block thicknesses is needed.

Recommended research questionHow does angle of incidence affect the lateral displacement of a light ray passing through a rectangular acrylic block of fixed thickness?

Equipment

Rectangular acrylic block, ray box or low-power school laser, white paper, ruler, protractor, pencil.

Key physics

\[d=t\,\frac{\sin(i-r)}{\cos r}\]

Use Snell's law, \(n_1\sin i=n_2\sin r\), to relate \(i\) and \(r\). The block thickness \(t\) is kept constant.

Method

  1. Place the block on paper, trace around it and draw a normal at a fixed entry point.
  2. Set a chosen angle of incidence and send a narrow ray through the block.
  3. Mark the incident and emergent ray positions accurately.
  4. Remove the block and draw the ray paths.
  5. Extend the original incident direction and measure the perpendicular lateral displacement to the emergent ray.
  6. Repeat the measurement and then repeat for at least six incidence angles.
  7. Keep the same block, entry point and wavelength/light source throughout.

Useful analysis

Compare measured displacement with the model calculated from the block thickness and refractive index. Discuss angular uncertainty, ray width, alignment and whether uncertainty grows at larger angles.

Safety: follow your school's laser/ray-box procedures. Keep beams away from eye level and never look directly into a laser beam.
IV: incidence angleDV: lateral displacementFixed block thickness
