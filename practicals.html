PRACTICAL 01 • A.1 / A.3 / A.4

Rolling ball: energy and release height

Use a simple ramp but analyse where the gravitational energy goes.

Example research question — your own must be differentHow does the vertical release height of a solid sphere affect the fraction of its initial gravitational potential energy converted into translational kinetic energy at the bottom of a fixed ramp?

Equipment

Ramp, solid sphere, metre rule, balance, blocks/stand, tape or markers, light gate or slow-motion video with a scale.

Data booklet starting equations

Ek=12mv2
ΔEp=mgΔh

Use these A.3 equations to derive your own comparison between the starting gravitational energy and the measured translational kinetic energy. Do not simply copy a pre-made ratio.

Method

  1. Fix the ramp angle and mark the point where speed will be measured near the bottom.
  2. Choose a release point and measure its vertical height above the bottom measurement point.
  3. Release the same sphere from rest without pushing it.
  4. Measure its speed at the bottom using a light gate or calibrated slow-motion video.
  5. Repeat the measurement several times and find the mean speed.
  6. Repeat for at least six well-spaced vertical release heights.
  7. Use the data-booklet energy equations to calculate the quantities required by your own derived relationship, including appropriate uncertainties.

Build your analysis

Derive a suitable relationship from the two energy equations, then decide what graph would test whether the energy behaviour changes with release height. If you compare with an ideal rolling-sphere prediction, explain and reference any extra rotational-physics relationship you use.

PRACTICAL 02 • A.1 / A.2

Trolley on a ramp: real vs ideal acceleration

Instead of only verifying a = g sin θ, investigate how the real system departs from the model.

Example research question — your own must be differentHow does incline angle affect the percentage difference between the measured acceleration of a trolley and the theoretical acceleration predicted for a frictionless incline?

Equipment

Dynamics trolley, ramp, blocks/stand, inclinometer or protractor, motion sensor / light gates / video analysis, metre rule.

Data booklet starting equations

F=ma
Fg=mg
AV=Asinθ

Use the A.2 force equations and the booklet vector component relationship to derive the frictionless acceleration expected along the slope.

Method

  1. Set the ramp to a small angle and measure θ accurately.
  2. Use the same trolley and the same release position for every run.
  3. Release the trolley from rest without pushing it.
  4. Determine acceleration using your chosen sensor or video method.
  5. Repeat and calculate a mean acceleration.
  6. Repeat for at least six ramp angles across a safe, practical range.
  7. Use the data-booklet equations to derive the theoretical acceleration for each angle, then compare it with the measured value.

Build your analysis

Derive the theoretical acceleration from the booklet equations first. Then choose a graph that tests the model and shows how any disagreement changes across your angle range. Use the gradient, intercept and uncertainty as evidence rather than being told in advance what they should be.

IV: incline angleDV: % differenceSame trolley + track
PRACTICAL 03 • A.1 / A.3

Bouncing ball: coefficient of restitution

Simple setup, but enough quantitative analysis to investigate how collision behaviour changes.

Example research question — your own must be differentHow does the initial drop height of a basketball affect its coefficient of restitution when bouncing from a fixed hard surface?

Equipment

Ball, vertical measuring scale, phone with slow-motion video, tripod/support, flat hard surface.

Data booklet starting equations

v2=u2+2as
ΔEp=mgΔh

The coefficient of restitution definition is not supplied in the data booklet. Define and reference it in your background physics, then use the booklet equations to connect the measured heights with the motion.

Method

  1. Fix a vertical scale beside the bounce position and place the camera perpendicular to the motion.
  2. Choose a drop height and use the same reference point on the ball each time.
  3. Release the ball without spin or an extra push.
  4. Use the video to measure the maximum height of the first rebound.
  5. Repeat several times at the same height.
  6. Repeat for at least six drop heights.
  7. Use your referenced definition of coefficient of restitution and the relevant data-booklet equations to derive the calculation you will apply to each height.

Build your analysis

Derive the form of the coefficient-of-restitution calculation from your definition and the measured heights. Then decide how best to test whether it changes with drop height and whether any change is larger than the uncertainty.

PRACTICAL 04 • C.1

Pendulum: where the small-angle model breaks down

Use the familiar pendulum in a less routine way by testing the limit of a model.

Example research question — your own must be differentHow does initial angular displacement affect the percentage deviation of the measured period of a simple pendulum from the period predicted by the small-angle model?

Equipment

Retort stand and clamp, string, pendulum bob, metre rule, protractor, stopwatch or preferably video.

Data booklet starting equations

T=1f=2πω
T=2πlg

The C.1 pendulum expression is already given in the booklet. Your job is to use it as the small-angle model and design a quantitative way to test where the measured behaviour begins to depart from it.

Method

  1. Build a pendulum with fixed length L and measure L from pivot to the centre of the bob.
  2. Choose a starting angle, beginning with a small value such as 5°.
  3. Release the bob without pushing it.
  4. Measure the time for multiple complete oscillations and calculate the period.
  5. Repeat to obtain a reliable mean period.
  6. Repeat for a wide range of initial angles, extending beyond the small-angle range.
  7. Calculate the percentage deviation from T0 at each angle.

Build your analysis

Use the data-booklet period as the model prediction. Define a justified measure of disagreement, then choose a graph that shows how that disagreement changes with initial angle. Use uncertainty to judge whether the departure from the model is meaningful.

IV: initial angleDV: % deviation from T₀Fixed length + bob
PRACTICAL 05 • C.2 / C.4

Standing waves: resonant harmonics

Use the signal generator to explore how the resonant frequencies are organised while keeping the string and tension fixed.

Example research question — your own must be differentHow does harmonic number affect the resonant frequency of a string with fixed vibrating length and fixed tension?

Equipment

Signal generator, vibration generator, string, pulley, mass hanger and masses, metre rule, balance if linear density must be measured.

Data booklet starting equations

v=fλ
T=1f

The wavelength condition for each standing-wave harmonic is not written in the data booklet. Work it out from the node/antinode pattern and combine it with v = fλ to derive the relationship you will test.

Method

  1. Set a fixed vibrating length of string between the vibrator and pulley.
  2. Use a fixed hanging mass so the string tension stays constant.
  3. Adjust the signal-generator frequency until the fundamental standing-wave pattern is clear.
  4. Record the resonant frequency and identify the harmonic from the node/antinode pattern.
  5. Increase the frequency to find several higher harmonics without changing the string length or tension.
  6. Repeat each resonance measurement so you can estimate the uncertainty in locating the resonant frequency.
  7. Keep the string type, vibrating length and hanging mass unchanged throughout.

Build your analysis

Derive the expected relationship between harmonic number and resonant frequency from the data-booklet wave equation and the standing-wave geometry. Then choose a graph that tests that relationship. Discuss how precisely resonance and the vibrating length can be identified.

IV: harmonic number nDV: resonant frequencyFixed tension, L and string
PRACTICAL 06 • C.2 / C.4

Resonance tube: spacing between resonances

Using two resonant lengths reduces the effect of the uncertain open-end correction.

Example research question — your own must be differentHow does driving frequency affect the separation between the first and second resonant lengths of a closed air column?

Equipment

Resonance tube with adjustable water level, metre rule, speaker/phone, signal generator or tone-generator app, stand/clamp.

Data booklet starting equations

v=fλ
T=1f

The closed-pipe resonance-length condition is not supplied in the booklet. Derive it from the standing-wave pattern, then combine it with v = fλ.

Method

  1. Choose a driving frequency and position the speaker close to the open end of the tube.
  2. Change the air-column length slowly until the first clear resonance is found; record L1.
  3. Continue changing the length until the next clear resonance is found; record L2.
  4. Repeat the pair of measurements to estimate the uncertainty in locating resonance.
  5. Calculate ΔL = L2 − L1.
  6. Repeat for several frequencies for which both resonances fit within the tube.
  7. Keep the same tube and speaker arrangement.

Build your analysis

Use the booklet wave equation and the closed-pipe geometry to derive how resonance spacing should depend on frequency. Choose a graph that tests your derived relationship, then use the fitted relationship to determine a physical quantity such as the speed of sound and compare it with a referenced value.

IV: frequencyDV: ΔLSame tube + conditions
PRACTICAL 07 • C.3

Refraction: lateral displacement through one block

No collection of different block thicknesses is needed.

Example research question — your own must be differentHow does angle of incidence affect the lateral displacement of a light ray passing through a rectangular acrylic block of fixed thickness?

Equipment

Rectangular acrylic block, ray box or low-power school laser, white paper, ruler, protractor, pencil.

Data booklet starting equation

n1n2=sinθ2sinθ1=v2v1

The booklet gives the refraction relationship, but it does not give a lateral-displacement formula. Use Snell's law and the geometry of the ray through the block to derive the expression needed for your investigation.

Method

  1. Place the block on paper, trace around it and draw a normal at a fixed entry point.
  2. Set a chosen angle of incidence and send a narrow ray through the block.
  3. Mark the incident and emergent ray positions accurately.
  4. Remove the block and draw the ray paths.
  5. Extend the original incident direction and measure the perpendicular lateral displacement to the emergent ray.
  6. Repeat the measurement and then repeat for at least six incidence angles.
  7. Keep the same block, entry point and wavelength/light source throughout.

Build your analysis

Derive a geometrical model for lateral displacement using the data-booklet refraction equation. Then choose how to compare your measured displacement with the prediction across the angle range. Discuss angular uncertainty, ray width and alignment.

Safety: follow your school's laser/ray-box procedures. Keep beams away from eye level and never look directly into a laser beam.
